The gates
---------

1. "Zero spawns before `cc` for a one-module edit of hello world."
   MET for the build graph, MISSED by one process overall.

   `nimony c --report` after appending one line to a one-module program:

     nifmake-report nimsem=1 total=1 inproc=1
     nifmake-report cc=1 dceEmit=1 dceLive=0 hexer=1 lengc=1 link=1 total=5 inproc=3

   Every node of both graphs before `cc` ran in this process. The one process
   left is `nifler`, and it is not a DAG node here at all: `deps.execNifler`
   re-parses the edited module during dependency discovery, before the graph
   is emitted. Nifler is the one frontend phase that could not be registered —
   linking it needs `--path:$nim/compiler`, which collides
   `compiler/platform.nim` with `src/lib/platform.nim` under one object-file
   name and breaks the link on `CPU`/`OS`; notes/a2b.md §3.2 and the header of
   `src/nimony/phases.nim` carry the full finding.

2. "A new CTFE evaluation with `--ctfe:engine` <= 0.2 s, and the frontend of it
   spawn-free."
   NOT MEASURABLE HERE for the engine half; the spawn-free half is MET.

   This machine has no `../nativenif` checkout, so nimsem is built without
   `-d:nimonyEngine` and `--ctfe:engine` falls back to the subprocess for every
   evaluation (`tests/ctfe_diff` says so and skips its own engine pair). What
   can be measured is the subprocess path, and it moved:

     ctfe.cold   2.539 s -> 2.187 s wall, 3.804 s -> 2.470 s cpu
     ctfe.edit   0.197 s -> 0.072 s wall  (five `const`s, all memo hits)
     bench.cold  6.028 s -> 4.679 s wall, 9.296 s -> 4.129 s cpu (14 `const`s)

   Spawn-free is asserted rather than timed, by `incrementalInprocTests`
   phase 4: after compiling `tests/incremental/ctfedep.nim` into a fresh
   nimcache, `<nimcache>/ledger.nif` holds no `spawn` sample for `nimsem`,
   `hexer`, `dce`, `dceLive`, `dceEmit` or `lengc` and does hold one for
   `cc`/`link`. That covers the sub-compile's own two build graphs as well as
   the outer build's, because both write into the same nimcache.

   `--stats` on a hello world shows the same thing directly:

     [stats] phase       samples   produce ms   spawn ms
     [stats] cc                4          0.0       53.9
     [stats] dceEmit           4          0.2        0.0
     [stats] dceLive           1          9.2        0.0
     [stats] hexer             4         11.6        0.0
     [stats] lengc             4          0.5        0.0
     [stats] link              1          0.0      197.1
     [stats] nifler           30          0.4        5.2
     [stats] nimsem            4         18.7        0.0

3. "Cold stdlib-wide build within 10 % of `--spawn:always` wall time."
   MET: 5.516 s against 5.488 s, +0.5 %.

   This is the gate the scheduling rule was tuned against. The literal
   per-node form of JIT.md 6.2's cost clause answers "not worth a process" for
   each of 99 cheap `hexer` nodes at one DAG depth and then runs them one
   after another — 0.606 s of `hexer` plus 0.776 s of `nimsem` serialised,
   measured with `nimony c -f --profile`, and stdlib.cold 11 % over. Scaling
   the clause to the depth (`est * readyAtDepth < spawn * k * cores`, which
   reduces to the written rule at `readyAtDepth == cores`) recovers it. See
   the commit f281154d and notes/a2b.md.


The one regression
------------------

`stdlib.forced` is +15 % wall against both base and `--spawn:always`, while
costing 20 % less CPU. A forced rebuild of 101 stdlib modules re-runs every
`nimsem` node, and those sit in an import chain whose depths are mostly
narrower than the 10 cores — so JIT.md 6.2's second clause ("fewer ready nodes
than cores -> in-process") keeps them here and they run one after another,
where the fan-out would have used the two or three cores a narrow depth can
fill. It is the cost of in-process phases being sequential, which JIT.md 6.1
takes on deliberately and defers until a ledger shows threads would pay.

It is not a dev-loop scenario: `stdlib.edit`, which is, is +4.5 % wall for
-56 % cpu, and every hello/ctfe/bench scenario is faster on both axes.
